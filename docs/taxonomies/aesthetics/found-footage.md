# Found Footage

Found Footage serves as the aesthetic framework for fictional narratives presented as recovered, discovered, or assembled recordings made from within the events themselves. It provides the visual, structural, and tonal identity for stories told through diegetic cameras: camcorders, surveillance systems, news footage, webcams, police recordings, cell phones, or any other recording device that exists inside the story world rather than outside it. The Found Footage aesthetic does not change what kind of story is being told. A Horror narrative remains Horror, a Thriller remains Thriller, and a Drama remains Drama. What Found Footage changes is the mode of access: the audience is positioned not as viewer of a staged film but as witness to material that was ostensibly captured by participants, survivors, investigators, or institutions after the fact.

## Core Characteristics of Found Footage Aesthetic:

### Diegetic Camera Presence

The defining condition of Found Footage is that the camera exists within the story world. Someone is holding it, wearing it, setting it up, monitoring it, or retrieving it. This gives every image a fictional source and turns filming itself into part of the dramatic situation. Characters must decide when to keep recording, when to stop, and why the camera remains present even when events become dangerous. That diegetic presence is what separates Found Footage from conventional handheld cinematography, which may feel raw or unstable without ever pretending the camera is part of the story.

### Recovered or Assembled Evidence Framing

Found Footage typically presents itself as material that has been discovered after the events occurred or assembled from multiple recordings to reconstruct them. This evidentiary framing is central to the aesthetic's power. The audience is invited to treat the images as traces of something that happened, even while fully understanding the work is fictional. Title cards, opening disclaimers, institutional logos, police or news framing devices, and edits justified as evidence compilation all reinforce the sense that the footage has an afterlife beyond the moment of capture.

### Limited Perspective and Incomplete Visibility

Because the camera is inside the event rather than outside it, Found Footage embraces restricted knowledge. The frame is partial, unstable, and often badly aimed. Important actions happen off-camera, in darkness, behind walls, or at the edge of the lens. This is not merely a budgetary limitation or stylistic flourish. It is a core part of the aesthetic framework: the audience knows only what these cameras could plausibly record, and fear, tension, or intimacy emerge from the gap between what is happening and what can actually be seen.

### Raw, Degraded, or Functional Image Quality

Found Footage often adopts the visual language of consumer or institutional recording rather than polished cinematography. The footage may be grainy, overexposed, underlit, digitally compressed, glitched, improperly framed, or interrupted by signal loss. These qualities are not defects to be corrected but part of the presentation layer. The visual language communicates immediacy, contingency, and the sense that what matters is not beauty but capture. Even higher-budget Found Footage works retain this functional quality by preserving the logic of real recording devices rather than conventional camera omniscience.

### Performance Shaped by Recording

Characters in Found Footage do not behave as though they are being observed by an invisible film apparatus. They behave as people aware of a camera's presence. They play to it, ignore it, hide from it, argue over it, and use it to mediate fear or control. This creates a different performance texture than conventional fiction. Dialogue often feels more interrupted, more overlapping, more provisional, because the characters' relationship to the act of recording is itself part of the scene's meaning.

### Authenticity Through Procedural Recording Logic

Found Footage relies on the audience believing, moment to moment, that these images could have been captured this way. The aesthetic therefore depends on procedural logic: why this device is here, who had time to set it up, why it continues running, why this clip exists, why this angle is available, and who later assembled the material. When that logic is persuasive, the aesthetic achieves its distinctive effect of fictional authenticity. When it fails, the illusion collapses. More than most aesthetics, Found Footage depends on internal recording logic as part of its presentation framework.

## Narrative Genres Layered Over Found Footage Aesthetic

While Found Footage provides the recording framework, the narrative genre dictates how the story unfolds:

- **Found Footage Horror:** Fear-based stories presented as recordings captured by victims, investigators, or participants whose footage becomes evidence of what overtook them. The aesthetic intensifies Horror by limiting visibility, restricting knowledge, and making the camera itself part of the vulnerability.

- **Found Footage Thriller:** Suspense narratives where surveillance, pursuit, conspiracy, or escalating danger are presented through assembled recordings, emphasizing uncertainty, fragmentation, and incomplete evidence.

- **Found Footage Drama:** Character-driven stories where the presence of cameras shapes intimacy, confession, self-performance, and the emotional meaning of events, allowing personal breakdown or relationship conflict to be captured as lived record.

- **Found Footage Science Fiction:** Speculative narratives presented through mission logs, surveillance archives, amateur recordings, or recovered transmissions, using the aesthetic's evidentiary quality to make impossible events feel documented rather than staged.

## Examples of Found Footage Aesthetic

### The Blair Witch Project:

Demonstrates Found Footage Horror in its purest form, where student filmmakers' own cameras become the only means by which the audience experiences the disappearance, fear, and escalating breakdown of their expedition. The limited perspective, degraded image quality, and constant question of why the camera keeps running create the film's specific terror.

### As Above, So Below:

Represents Found Footage Horror-Adventure, where an expedition into the Paris catacombs uses headlamps, handheld cameras, and documentary-style exploration footage to make descent itself feel evidentiary and immediate. The aesthetic's diegetic camera logic and limited visibility turn tunnels, chambers, and off-screen spaces into active sources of dread, while the recovered-record framing makes the characters' movement deeper into the labyrinth feel like material preserved from an expedition that should not have continued.

### Cloverfield:

Shows Found Footage Action-Horror, where a large-scale urban disaster is presented through the unstable perspective of one consumer camcorder, making spectacle feel immediate and disorienting because the audience has no access to a stable overview of the event.

### Chronicle:

Exemplifies Found Footage Science Fiction-Drama, where teenagers recording themselves gradually transform the camera from social play into a record of power, alienation, and psychological unraveling. The aesthetic makes the speculative premise feel personally documented rather than externally narrated.

## As an Aesthetic Framework

Found Footage serves as the presentation layer for fictional narratives that want the audience to experience events as recovered record rather than conventional staged observation. It can combine with other aesthetics: a Found Footage Horror may also be Contemporary if set in an ordinary present-day social world, or Near Future if the recording ecosystem depends on surveillance infrastructure and advanced devices. Found Footage does not determine the story's themes or narrative backbone. It determines how the audience is allowed to see, know, and believe. Its power lies in replacing cinematic omniscience with fictional evidence, creating the sensation that the story was not told to the audience so much as left behind for them to discover.
